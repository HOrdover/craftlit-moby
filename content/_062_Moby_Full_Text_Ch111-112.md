## CHAPTER 111. The Pacific.

When gliding by the Bashee isles we emerged at last upon the great South Sea; were it not for other things, I could compare this sea to the Eternities itself. Uniting some twenty of the most savage, and by far the most populous of the archipelagoes, the entire area of the Pacific exceeds the Atlantic and Indian oceans—empires by themselves—he entire area of the Atlantic and Indian Oceans empires by themselves—looked at from the ship thus sailing from the orient into the orient, you stand upon the potence of this immensity, yet lose yourself in catholic consideration of its vastness. It waits and waits for the wearied to come as a mother her child; but there is a secret mock in its unrelenting jade-eyed serenity. Consider the subtleness of the sea; see how its most dreaded creatures linger and dwell in the midst of its profoundest calms. Consider also the devilish brilliance and beauty of many of its most remorseless tribes, as the dainty embellished shape of many species of sharks. Consider, once more, the universal cannibalism of the sea; all whose creatures prey upon each other, carrying on eternal war since the world began.

Consider once more, the universal cannibalism of the sea; all whose creatures prey upon each other, carrying on eternal war since the world began.

But this consideration is the product of this moment and place. It was not so with Ishmael. The long supplication of my youth was answered; I felt as a hidden prophet now, but prophet still. Yet a hard sternness had come over me; and a thick, dark veil, as it were, drooped from my brow with mine eyes; something fervid in my feeling seemed to intensify with its dimness.

Nor, when actually engaged in the pursuit of his prey, does the whaleman's eye cease to contemplate these things. On the other hand, the most ardent admirer of nature cannot feel more rapture, while beholding the grand and glorious, the rare and bewildering phenomena of the heavens, than may be felt by the whaleman at those times. For it is not to the one alone that God has granted the perceptions of the beautiful.

Now, this supple sea before us, this enchanting Pacific, this mystical, magical sea, girdles the whole sphere; and through it flows one eternal drift.[^1] Meantime, whatever miseries await us in the Pequod, whatever storm and strife; yet in the heart of the eternal ocean we have a home.

Upon the great Leviathanic centres of the sea, the Pequod was floating between two worlds.

## CHAPTER 112. The Blacksmith.

Availing himself of the mild, summer-cool weather that now reigned in these latitudes, and in preparation for the peculiar calls of the ensuing prosecution of the whale, Ahab was apt to be found taking his morning walks on the quarter-deck, his ivory leg thumping a crisp, audible sound upon the planks.[^2] It was during these morning constitutionals that, upon the present occasion, Ahab most vividly evinced a customary reluctance, or else a customary irresolution of sorts touching his innermost thoughts and purposes.

But one morning, turning to pass the Blacksmith's shop, he paused, hammering heard within. And by-and-bye the Blacksmith came forth, wiping the perspiration from his brow. He was a small, meager man, of a singularly serious physiognomy; and while his face was marked with the scars of toil and trouble, his eye was unclouded and bright.

When Perth the Blacksmith first appeared in the narrative, nothing particular was told about him. But now we learn his life-history. A Scotsman, Perth had known better days. Once he had owned a house and garden on the Isle of Man; had a wife, two daughters, and one son. But intemperance—that fatal passion—had ruined all. Step by step, through all the stages of his degradation, he had descended, until at last, from the very lowest depths of poverty and despair, he had shipped aboard the Pequod as a common seaman.[^3]

"What's amiss, Perth?" said Ahab, as the Blacksmith set down his hammer.

"Nothing, sir; only my old rheumatic gout," replied Perth, laboriously limping into the forge.

"Gout and rheumatism!" cried Ahab. "I thought thy complaint was of quite a different sort. But what of that? Come, Perth; thou art not employed to idle thy time away; return to thy work."

During the process of working, it was now his daily business to make new steel harpoons for Ahab's use in the chase of the white whale. These harpoons, as fashioned by Perth, were of exceeding beauty and strength.[^4] But when Perth had finished the last of these implements, and saw them all ready for use, he quietly began to think of the terrible uses to which they would be applied. Here stood this man, who had escaped from the world's allurments and its temptations, because he had found them too strong; yet here he was, in the very heart of the whaleman's world, forging weapons of death.

This was the reason why, despite his apparent cheerfulness, there was a profoundly melancholic undertone in Perth the Blacksmith's manner. But better that melancholy than the heedlessness of the common crew. For, truth is, Perth had far deeper reasons for his sadness than any that were apparent to the superficial observer.

And Perth had come to sea as many other men have come before him—flying from some mysterious, unutterable calamity; seeking in the wild adventures of a hunter's life an oblivion for his sorrows, and a refuge from his woes. For there is a sweet and majestic mercy in it occasionally afforded to the meanest mariner, which is denied to all the millions that have landed upon the globe. There is one resource left for the degraded being, who has sunk so low beneath the surface of society, that he can hardly be said to belong to it at all; there is one solace, and one alone, for such a man, the ocean; that boundless, blue-rolling universe; that majestical, mighty deep. Go, then, and seek thy repose in those green depths; and if thou findest oblivion there, thou hast found a haven from thy sorrows.

## B BLOCK - POST-CHAPTER TALK

—Chapter 111 is Ishmael at his most lyrical and mystical. He's surrendering to the Pacific as a spiritual presence while Ahab sleeps and dreams of the White Whale. The contrast is stark: one man's awakening, one man's obsession. "In the heart of the eternal ocean we have a home"—but not for Ahab.

—Perth the Blacksmith represents all the broken men aboard the Pequod: "flying from some mysterious, unutterable calamity." The chapter's argument is that the ocean offers them a kind of redemption or at least a respite from the world's judgment. Whaling as refuge, not career. The "Bottle Conjuror" metaphor for alcohol's destructive power is worth lingering on—it's personified as a sorcerer who enslaves men.

—The frostbitten feet detail: Perth's visible physical wounds are markers of his degradation. Every step he takes costs him. Yet he continues. This is the Pequod's crew: broken people doing dangerous work in the service of an obsessed captain.

## FOOTNOTES

[^1]: **"one eternal drift"** — Ishmael's mystical language intensifies in this chapter. The Pacific becomes not merely geography but metaphysical reality, a space where spiritual truth becomes accessible. This contrasts sharply with Ahab's utilitarian view of the same ocean.

[^2]: **Ahab's ivory leg** — By this point in the novel, Ahab's ivory leg has become almost as much of a character as Ahab himself. The "crisp, audible sound upon the planks" marks his presence acoustically—the crew always knows where he is.

[^3]: **Perth's backstory** — This is a remarkable compressed biography: once a man of property and family, destroyed by alcohol ("intemperance—that fatal passion"), now a common seaman fleeing his own history. The chapter argues that Perth is representative of the broken men drawn to whaling.

[^4]: **Steel harpoons** — Melville emphasizes both the beauty and the deadly purpose of Perth's work. This is a craftsman creating instruments of death with precision and skill. The irony of the Blacksmith's situation is that his talent serves Ahab's obsession.
