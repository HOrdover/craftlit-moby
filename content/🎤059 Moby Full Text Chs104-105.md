## CHAPTER 104. The Fossil Whale.

From his mighty bulk the whale affords a most congenial theme whereon to enlarge, amplify, and generally expatiate. Would you, you could not compress him. By good rights he should only be treated of in imperial folio. Not to tell over again his furlongs from spiracle to tail, and the yards he measures about the waist; only think of the gigantic involutions of his intestines, where they lie in him like great cables and hawsers coiled away in the subterranean orlop-deck of a line-of-battle-ship.[^1]

And as for his tail, how at all fain am I to stagger to this emprise under the weightiest words of the dictionary,[^2] and make him only to wail the more for her he has lost. But I am not a bard. This is prose, and I must not be guilty of such extravagance. Yet even in prose, one may get a bit of poetry. The ribs and terrors in the whale, arched over me a dismal gloom, while all God's sun-lit waves rolled by, and lift me deepening down to doom.

Ere entering upon the subject of Fossil Whales, I present my credentials as a geologist, by stating that in my miscellaneous time I have been a stone-mason, and also a great digger of ditches, canals and wells, wine-vaults, cellars, and cisterns of all sorts; and have freely offered all the general services I could.[^3] It is to be noted that in this light, the matter of the Fossil Whale is of a piece with those other vexed questions of Geology, Natural History, the Theory of Species, and the like—questions in regard to which Science herself is but a confused mass of notions.

The Cetacea include whales, dolphins, and porpoises. According to the best learned naturalists, among whom I must not omit to mention the great French comparative anatomist, Baron Cuvier,[^4] the Sperm Whale is antediluvian in his proportions.[^5] The whale is pre-Adamite.[^6] Shem himself is a late-comer when compared to the leviathan.[^7]

Now, from this it plainly appears that the whale has been fossiliferous[^8] for a very long time—longer, indeed, than any historian has supposed. And though the seas have since drowned many a city, many a continent, many a high-arching mountain—still the whale has survived; still swims the sea. Friends, hold my arms![^9]

But I must be content with only one example of the remarkable fossil whale. I refer the reader to that most excellent of all sources, Baron Cuvier,[^4] who has established beyond all doubt that among the fossil Cetacea, the Sperm Whale is the oldest. The earliest Cetacea did not come up to even the present Sperm Whale in magnitude—a fact that destroys all idea of diminution in size over geologic time, and instead suggests the whale has always been the whale.

This formidable creature, once living, was of enormous size. But I now refer the reader to the great Basilosaurus.[^10] He was found in the year 1832, in the State of Alabama. This fossil was for a time supposed to have been a reptile. Richard Harlan, an American naturalist, named this creature Basilosaurus, which name signifies King Lizard.[^10] But the great English comparative anatomist, Professor Owen, subsequently proved this creature to have been a Whale; insomuch that Owen rechristened the monster Zeuglodon; and in his paper read before the London Geological Society, pronounced it, in substance, one of the most extraordinary creatures that the mutations of the globe have yet discovered.[^10]

The assembled skeleton that Koch exhibited measured one hundred and fourteen feet—but this was afterwards ascertained to be a compound of the bones of different individuals.[^11] In truth, the real Zeuglodon measured somewhat over fifty feet in length, which is still tremendous.

Now, from all this, what must we conclude? To produce a mighty book, you must choose a mighty theme. No great and enduring volume can ever be written on the flea, though many there be who have tried it.[^12]

Is it not curious, that so vast a being as the whale should see the world through so small an eye, and hear the thunder through an ear which is smaller than a hare's? But, if his eyes were broad as the lens of Herschel's great telescope; and his ears capacious as the porches of cathedrals; would that make him any longer of sight, or sharper of hearing? Not at all.—Why then do you try to "enlarge" your mind? Subtilize it. And the universe's suburbs[^13] are where such subtilizing leads you—out to the edges of creation, where the big words and the small facts live together.

Deep in the temples of the Arsacidean isles,[^14] and in the Temple of the Holy Evangelists at the Burton-Constable estate of Sir Clifford Constable,[^15] and in the Nantucket Historical Association—in all these places have I seen the skeleton of the Sperm Whale. And more than once I have been a contemplative vagabond through these mansions of the dead; musing upon the fact that in the history of the race, these whale skeletons have not always been treated as mere museum curiosities—that they have been worshipped, not unworthily, as things most marvelous.

In the crotched top of this cavern, there was a crude sketch of the whale, as some Pharaonic artist, in an idle moment, had amused himself in tracing with his hieroglyphic chisel. And standing there in Pharaoh's time, you would not easily have recognized the creature by this sketch.[^16] Even as the great Temple at Denderah[^16] shows us a [Zodiac](https://preview.redd.it/dendera-zodiac-v0-gonx30h65p8f1.jpg?width=640&crop=smart&auto=webp&s=1af124dd85acf783f2b527a62f08fe924359437b) of celestial record—a planisphere of the heavens carved by Egyptian hands—so too the whale's own history is inscribed in stone and bone, if we know how to read it.

The whale is an enigma. He has been in the world ever since the time of Noah, and has survived the Flood—survived the destruction of that world, and all the changes which that destruction entailed. Pre-Adamite is the whale.[^6] The world is as old as it is. And the whale has been here quite as long.

I have been in Nantucket and elsewhere; I have visited the Galleries of Natural History in Paris, London, and Dresden; I have seen the skeleton of the whale in the British Museum, in the Museum at Edinburgh, in that at Rouen, in that at Berne, in Switzerland, and in various other places in Europe and America. I rejoice in my spine, as in the firm audacious staff of that flag which I fling half out to the world.

## CHAPTER 105. Does the Whale's Magnitude Diminish?—Will He Perish?

Inasmuch as in this nineteenth century, the science of cetology is in its infancy, they say; and it is not yet completed to the uttermost coil of the least worm; and that in things that have been stated by naturalists, many things remain to be determined—I cannot without great reason be expected to throw much definitive light upon the question of the diminishing magnitude of the whale.

In the first place, I wish to lay before you a list of the whales in the Tertiary system[^17] of the geologists—I mean the whales in the strata of the geologic history of whales, which are all now firmly established and accounted for. There is the Basilosaurus, already briefly mentioned in the preceding chapter; the Zeuglodon; the Delphinapterus; and the Delphinorhynchus. These are all fossil Cetacea; and from Cuvier's examination of them, it appears these ancient creatures were not any larger—in some cases were even smaller—than the great sperm whale of to-day.

So that, so far from there being any evidence of the diminishing magnitude of the whale, there is actually evidence of the very reverse.

And what is said of the Sperm Whale is true also of the Right Whale, and the Fin Whale, and the Sulphur-Bottom Whale. With all these creatures, the naturalists have made no measurements which show them to have been at any time larger than they now are. The "biggest whale I ever see'd," said an old whaleman, who had had a long experience among them, "was one that my boat's crew came near striking in the South Sea, and he was certainly upwards of one hundred feet long." That whaleman's measurement may have been somewhat more than the truth; for such was the bias of that generation to overestimate the size of large animals. But, for all that, I do not hesitate to say, from what I have seen and heard, that the great Sperm Whale averages upwards of sixty feet in length;[^18] and the great blue whale—for there are whales and whales—is considerably larger still.

In the length and magnitude of the great Sperm Whale, I believe there can be set down, without exaggeration, as follows: the length of the full-grown male Sperm Whale is from sixty to eighty-five feet;[^18] and the weight is from forty to ninety tons. But I have been told by one who has seen the Sperm Whale in all his magnitudes, from the smallest to the greatest, that there have been examples of Sperm Whales one hundred feet long; and that such a whale would weigh upwards of one hundred tons.

But the further question remains: Will the whale perish altogether?

In the third place, we must consider the fact that the whale is not easily killed. The killing of a whale is a most difficult and dangerous operation; and, even with all the appliances of modern science and art, it is a matter of great labor and peril. The difficulty and danger of whale-killing is such, that the number of whales killed in any given year is comparatively small, even as compared with the great total number of whales in existence.[^19]

But what, in fact, is the history of the buffalo? We all know it. The buffalo was once as numerous as the sands of the sea; but the spear of man[^20] has gone through him as through kine.[^21] He is now but a remnant of his former multitude. And shall the whale perish as the buffalo has perished? I think not; and for this reason: the buffalo was pursued by man over his entire range, on land; but the whale is pursued only in certain parts of the sea; and in those parts of the sea where he is not pursued, he multiplies without any interference from man.

There are Rope Walks and Thames Tunnels of whales,[^22] where the Right Whale is known to have been seen in considerable numbers; and where, in former times, they were taken in great numbers; and where, in the present century, they are rarely, if ever, seen. But this is not because the whales have been exterminated in those localities; it is because they have been frightened away, and have sought refuge in more remote and inaccessible parts of the ocean.[^23]

But will the whale never diminish? For, it must be remembered that not only does the whale supply his own fuel, but all the light he gives, he gives in his own country. In his famous pasture to expatiate in,[^24] the whale has only to go further and further and further yet. In his sunsets suns still rise.[^25]

But, for the Sperm Whale, I believe there is no immediate danger of extermination. The reason is this: the Sperm Whale is found in all parts of the ocean; and in those parts where he has been most pursued, and most killed, he has had the power, or the wisdom, to seek refuge in more inaccessible quarters. The Polar Seas, for instance, the remotest parts of the Pacific, and the Indian Ocean, are all full of Sperm Whales; and there he is safe from all pursuit, or nearly so.[^23]

And there are whalebone whales,[^26] too—the great Mysticeti, the Right Whale, the Bowhead, the Humpback, the great Blue Whale—all of them filter-feeders, all of them in somewhat different danger from the cachalot.[^26] The spear of man[^27] has not yet reached them all, and may not for some time yet.

And as for his eternal whiteness, and his vast bulk, and his unconquerable toughness of constitution; these, added to the stately, almost reverential length and dignity of his great God-head and the awful majesty of his great tail—when the whale shall vanish from the sea, he will never be forgotten; for he is deathless in his individuality, and undying in his species.

We account the whale immortal in his species, however perishable in his individuality. He swam the seas before the continents broke water; he once swam over the site of the Tuileries, and Windsor Castle, and the Kremlin. In Noah's flood he despised Noah's Ark; and if ever the world is to be again flooded, like the Netherlands, to kill off its rats, then the eternal whale will still survive, and rearing upon the topmost crest of the equatorial flood, spout his frothed defiance to the skies.[^28]

# Footnotes

[^1]: **Orlop-deck** — the lowest deck of a ship, just above the hold, where cables and ropes were coiled and stored. Melville uses the anatomy of the ship to describe the anatomy of the whale, layering one vast structure onto another.
[^2]: **"fain am I to stagger to this emprise under the weightiest words of the dictionary"** — Melville announces his strategy openly: he will use the biggest, most ponderous words available because the subject demands them. It's both earnest and comic — he's performing the labor of language as physical effort, staggering under the weight of vocabulary. Watch for this in *fossiliferous*, *antediluvian*, *pre-Adamite* — he's not showing off so much as insisting that big beings require big words. The word *emprise* (a chivalric undertaking or bold venture) adds another layer of mock-heroic self-promotion. The "dictionary" Melville would have had in mind is primarily Samuel Johnson's *A Dictionary of the English Language* (1755) — see below for more.
[^3]: **Melville as geologist** — Melville's "credentials" are a comic setup: he's worked as a stone-mason and ditch-digger, so clearly he understands earth and bones. The joke is in the absurdity of the claim, but there's a real point underneath: amateur naturalists and working men often made genuine geological discoveries in the 19th century. The field was young enough that a well-read, observant person could make real contributions.
[^4]: **Baron Georges Cuvier (1769–1832)** — the founding giant of comparative anatomy and paleontology. Cuvier established that species could go extinct — a revolutionary and disturbing idea. He classified the Cetacea (whales, dolphins, porpoises), determined they were not fish, and examined fossil marine mammals. Melville had read him. Cuvier's authority in this chapter is real — Melville is invoking actual science. See: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Cuvier
[^5]: **"antediluvian"** — literally "before the Flood" (Latin *ante* = before, *diluvium* = flood). In Melville's era this was technically a scientific term — the geological period before Noah's Flood, which naturalists of the day used as a rough chronological marker. It had already begun to broaden into the common meaning "ancient beyond reckoning." Melville deploys it with full awareness of both meanings. See: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antediluvian
[^6]: **"pre-Adamite"** — theologically charged in 1851. The "Pre-Adamite" theory held that humans (or creatures) existed before the biblical Adam — a deeply controversial idea that threatened orthodox readings of Genesis. Placing the whale in pre-Adamite time removes him from the Genesis timeline entirely. The whale predates *mankind* — predates *everything* we call human history. For many in Melville's audience this was a genuinely transgressive claim. See: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Adamite
[^7]: **Shem** — Noah's eldest son (the three sons are Shem, Ham, and Japheth). The name "Semitic" derives from Shem — the Semitic peoples are the nations traditionally descended from Shem in the Table of Nations (Genesis 10). Melville's reference is casual but specific: Shem represents the most ancient human genealogy, the post-Flood generation. The whale predates even this. See: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shem
[^8]: **"fossiliferous"** — yes, a real scientific term still in use. From Latin *fossilis* (dug up, from *fodere* = to dig) + *ferre* (to bear or carry). A fossiliferous rock is one that contains fossils. Melville is using genuine scientific vocabulary, not making up impressive-sounding words. This is what he means by "the weightiest words of the dictionary" — they have actual weight because they carry actual meaning. See: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil
[^9]: **"Friends, hold my arms!"** — this is vintage "hold my beer" energy. Melville is performing comic self-restraint: the subject is so vast, the temptation to go full rhetorical excess is so overwhelming, that he needs to be physically restrained. He's playing the role of the enthusiast who knows he's about to go too far — and inviting the reader to laugh with him at the spectacle. The joke works because he has, in fact, been going quite far already.
[^10]: **Basilosaurus / Zeuglodon** — Melville gets the history essentially right. Richard Harlan (1834) described the Alabama creature and named it *Basilosaurus* (= "king lizard," from Greek *basileus* = king + *sauros* = lizard) — he thought it was a reptile. Richard Owen (1839), the great British anatomist, proved it was a whale and proposed the name *Zeuglodon* (= "yoke-toothed"). Today it is known as *Basilosaurus* (Harlan's name has priority by the rules of taxonomic nomenclature) and is recognized as an extinct whale. The "king lizard" mistake produced a name that has stuck. The *basilisk* of Harry Potter — a giant serpentine creature that kills with its gaze — shares the same Greek root: *basileus* (king). Snake-king, whale-king, lizard-king — all from the same word for royal power. See: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilosaurus and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilisk
[^11]: **The assembled skeleton / Koch's fraud** — Melville is correct that Koch's reconstruction was a composite of multiple individuals, inflating the apparent size dramatically. Koch exhibited this "sea serpent" (which he called *Hydrarchos*) as a traveling spectacle in New York and Europe before scientists identified the composite nature of the skeleton. The corrected single-individual length was around 50–60 feet. This was a famous episode in early American paleontology.
[^12]: **"To produce a mighty book, you must choose a mighty theme"** — one of the most direct meta-fictional statements in the novel. Melville steps fully out of the narrative to announce what he is doing. The whale justifies the size and ambition of *Moby-Dick* itself. He is not writing a novel; he is writing a WHALE. Compare this to the earlier "Cetology" chapter and the accumulation of whale chapters as the novel proceeds — each one is another brick in this justification.
[^13]: **"universe's suburbs"** — beautiful bathos. Melville sends himself and the reader all the way out to the cosmic edge — the farthest reaches of creation — and then calls it *the suburbs*. The joke deflates his own grandiosity at exactly the moment it would tip into pretension. After all that staggering under weighty words, the universe turns out to have a cul-de-sac. This is Melville at his most comic: the joke lands through anticlimax.
[^14]: **Arsacidean isles / Tranquo** — Melville's invented geography for the South Pacific setting of Chapter 102, where Ishmael measured a whale skeleton woven through with vines and living things. "Arsacidean" is a play on "Arsacid" (the Parthian dynasty) crossed with Pacific island naming conventions — exotic, vaguely Near Eastern. Tranquo's kingdom is fiction, but the concept of a whale skeleton as living architecture is a genuine poetic image Melville builds throughout the skeleton quartet (Ch. 102-105).
[^15]: **Burton Constable** — a real Yorkshire estate. Sir Clifford Constable did possess a sperm whale skeleton, which Melville had read about. The detail is authentic local color — this isn't invented. The Constables were a prominent Catholic Yorkshire family, and the estate at Burton Constable Hall still exists. See: https://www.burtonconstable.com/ — And the "dictionary" Melville mentions earlier ()[^2] is almost certainly Samuel Johnson's *A Dictionary of the English Language* (1755), the great monument of English lexicography before the Oxford English Dictionary. Johnson famously wrote the entire dictionary nearly single-handedly. Melville owned a copy and refers to it elsewhere.
[^16]: **Temple of Denderah (Dendera)** — Melville references the Temple of Hathor at Dendera, Egypt, famous for the Dendera Zodiac — a massive stone ceiling carving showing the constellations and a remarkable early map of the sky. The zodiac was cut from the temple ceiling in 1821 by French engineers and is now in the Louvre in Paris (Room 12, Sully Wing).
[^17]: **"Tertiary system"** — a real geological term, correctly used by Melville. The Tertiary period covered roughly 66 to 2.6 million years ago — the age of mammals following the extinction of the dinosaurs. It was named by Italian geologist Giovanni Arduino in the 18th century and formalized by Charles Lyell in 1833. Melville's usage is precisely current for 1851. (The term was officially retired by the International Commission on Stratigraphy in 2004, replaced by "Paleogene" and "Neogene," but "Tertiary" is still used informally.) Fossil Cetacea from the Tertiary include the ancestors of modern whales. See: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertiary and https://www.britannica.com/science/Tertiary-Period
[^18]: **Melville's size measurements** — largely in the right ballpark, with some inflation. The modern confirmed maximum length for a sperm whale is around 67-68 feet (a male taken in 1950). Melville's range of 60-85 feet is plausible for 1851 whaling, when the largest males had not yet been selectively hunted out. The jawbone in the Nantucket museum suggests 90-foot individuals may have existed. His weight figures (up to 90-100 tons) are higher than modern averages (35-45 tons for large males), but given that selective hunting has since removed the largest animals, Melville's era estimates may have been encountering genuinely larger specimens. See: https://www.whalepedia.net/faq/how-long-is-a-sperm-whale/
[^19]: **The whale-hunting math** — Melville's arithmetic about hunting efficiency is genuinely interesting. A typical whaling voyage: 40 men, 3-4 years, perhaps 40-80 whales total. Divided out, the kill rate per man per year is tiny compared to land hunting. This is the foundation of his argument that whales cannot be exterminated by this industry. He's right about the math of artisanal whaling. What he cannot foresee is the 1870 explosive harpoon gun (Svend Foyn), steam-powered factory ships, and the ability to process whales at sea — technologies that would change the math catastrophically.
[^20]: "spear of man"—Melville would have been watching the transition from spear/bow and arrow hunting of buffalo transition to rifle hunting - many more died once the new technology was introduced.
[^21]: **"kine"** — archaic plural of "cow," the language of the King James Bible. Melville uses it deliberately here to invoke Pharaoh's dream in Genesis 41: "seven fat kine" and "seven lean kine" that devour them — the dream that Joseph interprets as seven years of plenty followed by seven years of famine. The buffalo comparison glances at this archetype: once fat and numberless, now lean and diminishing. "Kine" marks the register as biblical, not merely agricultural. See: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cattle#Etymology
[^22]: **"Rope Walks and Thames Tunnels of Whales"** — a wonderful double image. A *ropewalk* is a long, narrow building (sometimes exceeding 1,000 feet) where rope was twisted and laid — a staple of every maritime city in the age of sail. The *Thames Tunnel* (opened 1843, designed by Marc Isambard Brunel and completed by his son Isambard Kingdom Brunel) was the first tunnel under a navigable river in the world, a great engineering wonder of Melville's era, famous for its dimensions. Melville is saying: the whales swim in passages as long and narrow as ropewalks, as wide as a great tunnel. The images are precise marine-industrial measurements, not vague decoration. See: https://www.ncpages.gov/ropewalks and https://ussconstitutionmuseum.org/2016/10/06/ropemakers-navy-part/
[^23]: **Whale behavioral adaptation** — Melville is making a remarkable argument for 1851: that whales, under hunting pressure, *learned* to seek refuge in more inaccessible waters — the Polar Seas, the remote Pacific. He is describing adaptive behavioral response to human predation. Modern cetology has confirmed that whales do exhibit learned behavioral adaptation: populations shift ranges, alter migration patterns, change social behaviors in response to hunting pressure. Melville was largely alone in 1851 in framing this as whale intelligence/wisdom rather than mere chance. This is a prescient behavioral ecology hypothesis, not just rhetoric. See: https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/no-firm-fortress-no-retreat-reading-and-mourning-moby-dick-on-the-cusp-of-climate-catastrophe/
[^24]: **"pasture to expatiate in"** — Melville uses *expatiate* in its original spatial sense, from Latin *ex-* (out) + *spatiari* (to walk about freely, to roam). The whales have ocean to roam freely through — an open pasture without fences. The more modern meaning ("to write or speak at length") is a secondary derivation from the same root. Here the word physically describes whale movement through ocean space, not human verbosity. This is Melville the careful vocabulary-user: choosing the root meaning, not the evolved one.
[^25]: **"in his sunsets suns still rise"** — the whale supplies his own light (whale oil) so he brings his own "sunrise" with him...even during the "sunset" of Whale's time, there is still hope for the Whales—he's got a lot of "land" to roam in.
[^26]: **"whalebone whales" / "cachalot"** — Melville uses "whalebone whales" for what we now call baleen whales: Right Whales, Bowhead, Humpbacks, Blue Whales — the filter-feeders who strain krill and small fish through keratin plates (the "whalebone" or baleen). These are distinct from the toothed whales, including the Sperm Whale. *Cachalot* is the French and Portuguese word specifically for the Sperm Whale — Melville uses it as a technical distinction. See: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baleen_whale and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cachalot
[^27]: **"The Spear of Man"** — Melville's arithmetic is correct even if the weapon name is slightly anachronistic/figurative. By 1851, buffalo were being killed largely by rifles, not spears — and after 1870, Sharps and Remington Rolling Block rifles made industrial-scale slaughter possible. "Spear" is likely figurative (all hunting weapons) or a deliberate archaism invoking the ancient human predator. His larger mathematical point stands: the manpower and time required for artisanal whaling make rapid extinction impractical with the tools of 1851. See: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bison_hunting and https://www.outdoorlife.com/conservation/buffalo-slaughter-archive-feature/
[^28]: **What Melville got right:** The mathematics of artisanal whaling were genuinely unsustainable for rapid extinction — forty men, four years, small numbers of whales per voyage, in an enormous ocean. His behavioral adaptation argument is prescient. His conservation instinct was correct. **What he couldn't foresee:** Steam-powered factory ships, explosive harpoon guns (the Svend Foyn gun, 1870), and the 20th-century's capacity to hunt Antarctic populations at industrial scale.  Serious Melville scholars don't read Chapter 105 as evidence of Melville being incorrect; they read it as a man reasoning correctly from the tools and scale of his era.
